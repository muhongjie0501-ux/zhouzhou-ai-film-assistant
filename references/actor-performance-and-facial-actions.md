# Directing performance for a usable shot

Use this guide when a scene depends on character behavior, voice, a readable reaction, emotional complexity or interaction between performers. For ordinary movement with no such difficulty, use only the relevant advice. This is a filmmaking aid; it does not supply a psychological diagnosis, a certified facial-coding manual or a set of model parameters.

## Start from the approved scene

Find what the director needs the audience to understand at this point. Preserve the words, action, relationship and outcome already approved. Work with the character's immediate situation rather than adding a biography to justify a gesture.

A playable intention concerns another person or something in the scene: keeping a companion from leaving, finding out whether an answer is truthful, or making a request without revealing its importance. The character's current obstacle determines which approach is available. If the approach stops working, a different action may communicate the change more clearly than simply increasing volume.

When failure has a consequence in the established story, use that consequence to judge the pressure on the performance. Losing an opportunity, trust or practical help can affect pace, physical distance or willingness to speak. Do not invent danger, trauma or a new relationship to raise the stakes. A quiet observation may have no immediate loss at all.

Subtext can be carried by a mismatch between what is said and what the person is doing. Choose an observable mismatch relevant to this situation; do not write an invisible thought and expect the model to infer a performance. An approved practical activity can provide that evidence. It may continue, become less accurate or stop when attention changes. Keep the existing object and activity; introducing a new prop is a directing decision.

Do not force every performance to conceal emotion, break down and recover. An openly expressed feeling, a decision already made, or sustained composure may be the intended beat. The guide helps execute that choice rather than replacing it with a preferred dramatic arc.

## Carry character decisions between shots

Role ① owns approved recurring behavior and voice choices in the designated film project. Use [project workflow](project-workflow.md) for their storage and changes. Keep only facts that matter to an upcoming shot: established posture, movement rhythm, a relevant habit, vocal qualities, or a story condition affecting the body. This is not a compulsory character questionnaire.

Role ③ selects the part of that record the shot can use. A habit should fit the current task, posture and available hands; repeating it at every appearance makes it a mannerism. It may be omitted when it does not serve the scene. Do not make a seated person stand merely to reproduce a walking habit.

Separate a planned character choice from the observed state of a take. Persistent choices come from ①; immediate carryover comes from the relevant inspected footage and, when available, ⑤'s selected interval. Fatigue, tears, breathlessness, an occupied hand or an unfinished action may matter at the join. Do not turn a generation accident into the new character specification. Story development and elapsed time can justify change, but the affected choice must remain traceable to the director's decision.

For voice continuity, distinguish recognizable vocal qualities from this moment's delivery. Accent, register or vocal texture may persist while pace, emphasis, breath and loudness vary with the scene. Distance, room acoustics and obstruction can change what is heard without changing the speaker. A written voice description is guidance, not a verified voice match or a new platform control.

## Make the intended performance visible and audible

Choose the channels the camera and microphone can actually convey. Do not direct every part of the face merely because it can move.

- In a wide view, orientation, spacing, weight, travel, handling of objects and the voice usually carry more information than a small change around the eyes.
- In a medium view, hands and posture can work with a readable change in gaze or expression. Preserve a useful physical activity instead of automatically replacing it with a facial close-up.
- In a close view, select the facial area carrying the important change. Give attention a target and give the change a cause. An extreme close view usually needs less competing information elsewhere.

Use ordinary descriptions of visible movement. The following are ways to make a direction specific, not an emotion dictionary or a required list:

| What needs to be readable | Useful information to supply |
|---|---|
| Attention | The person or object being watched, whether the eyes or head move, and when attention returns or remains elsewhere |
| An expression around the eyes | A change in the eyelids or brow, its size and duration, and the event that motivates it |
| A mouth or jaw change | Whether speaking is continuing, where the change can occur without obstructing articulation, and whether it is released or held |
| Physical effort or restraint | The part of the body bearing weight or tension, the contact involved, and how breathing supports the action |
| A vocal change | The relevant word or pause, the audible change and whether the next phrase sustains it |

Describe only what this framing can resolve. Skin color, moisture, tears and catchlights depend on material, physiology and lighting as well as performance; do not treat them as automatic emotion switches. Preserve the established light sources and facial identity.

When a region needs more exact direction, use its observable movement rather than a coded score:

- For eyes and brows, distinguish a gaze shift from a change in the eyelid opening or brow position. A blink can interrupt attention or occur after a cue; continuous blinking or restless eye movement is not the default. Specify a higher, lower or sideward target when that distinction affects the eyeline.
- For lips and jaw, distinguish touching lips from firm pressure, a changing mouth corner from a smile spread across the face, and a released jaw from an actively opened mouth. Name the hold or release only when it is readable and compatible with speech.
- For head and neck, choose the relevant turn, tilt, rise, lowering, approach or withdrawal. Say whether the torso follows when that affects blocking. Neck tension should fit actual effort or restraint rather than changing the person's anatomy.
- For breath and body, an inhalation, incomplete exhalation, swallow, altered grip or transfer of weight can carry the beat. Relate it to the ongoing action; do not add all of these as general signs of emotion.

These choices are available in both subtle and broad performance. Specify the useful side, amount, duration and release without prescribing a named emotion's supposedly correct face. A localized change may persist into the next beat, fade, recur or reverse according to the scene.

A facial movement does not prove what a real person feels or whether they are lying. Build the fictional performance from the approved context. Do not use a fixed smile, blink or gaze pattern as a universal truth test.

### Control timing without choreography overload

Where a change occurs matters as much as its size. Relate it to an audible word, a visible event, contact, recognition or a chosen pause. If nothing has yet reached the character's attention, preserve their previous behavior unless anticipation or a false reaction is explicitly intended.

For a difficult transition, distinguish the behavior before the cue, what changes when the cue is understood, and the state available for the next action or cut. These are reasoning questions, not required prompt headings. There is no obligatory number of intermediate beats.

Keep small actions small enough for the intended style but large enough for the chosen framing. Restrained work can rely on a single missed response or changed breath. Heightened, comic, theatrical or reality-show performance may require a broader, longer readable reaction. Use the director's chosen register; naturalism is not an automatic instruction to suppress it. Strong changes still need plausible movement, stable anatomy and sufficient time to be seen. Do not require a staggered sequence when synchronized behavior is the intended effect.

An optional attention choice is to let the gaze reach a relevant object before the head turns. For example, if the character is already carrying a tray, they may notice the empty chair without immediately changing the tray's direction. An eyes-only glance, a simultaneous turn or no turn can be equally appropriate. Never add the tray or chair solely to use this example.

Gesture timing also follows intention. In natural dialogue, a gesture need not illustrate every spoken word or finish at the end of every sentence. For an already approved gesture, it may complete while the person searches for the next phrase; it can also contradict the polite words being spoken. Pointing, counting, musical timing or deliberate unison may require exact coordination. Still hands remain a valid choice.

### Optional examples for developing a performance

These are invented scene situations showing how intention can affect observable behavior. Use an example only when its situation, activity and expressive scale already fit the approved scene; do not import its props, dialogue or events. They are not diagnostic signs of real emotion, model guarantees or required performance recipes.

- **Concealing pain while completing a task:** A mechanic signs the existing receipt with the uninjured hand, allowing the other hand to hang clear of the counter. When the customer reaches to shake it, the mechanic offers the signing hand instead and keeps the answer conversational.
- **Maintaining courtesy while wanting a conversation to end:** The host gives the visitor a complete, courteous answer while keeping one foot directed toward the already-open exit. When the visitor begins another question, the host pauses the departure rather than adding a fresh welcoming gesture.
- **Good news interrupted by a concern:** The athlete joins the team's applause, then notices the established message on the phone. The hands complete another clap while attention stays on the screen; the next spoken congratulations comes with less momentum.
- **Turning guilt into practical help:** A late-arriving worker sees the partner carrying the agreed equipment alone. The worker starts an explanation, abandons it on seeing the load tilt, and takes the available handle before trying to speak again.
- **Maintaining control under threat:** During the approved confrontation, the driver keeps both visible hands on the wheel and gives the requested short answer at an even volume. After the other person moves away, the driver attempts the next ordinary action and must adjust a grip that has become too tight.
- **Risking a request after hesitation:** The applicant holds the returned schedule long enough to read the empty space, then places it within the other person's reach while asking the approved question. The hand leaves the paper before the answer arrives, allowing the waiting face to carry the uncertainty.
- **Relief that retains physical fatigue:** After the last crate is safely set down, the mover takes the permitted rest without instantly regaining an upright, energetic posture. The breathing gradually becomes usable for speech while the arms still hang heavily.
- **Anger complicated by care:** During the established reprimand, the parent notices the loose fastening on the child's helmet and secures it. Keep the words firm; the careful handling and the unresolved argument are both present, without adding a reassuring smile that would settle the scene too early.
- **A broad comic recovery:** In an approved exaggerated scene, the presenter confidently addresses the wrong lectern, notices the waiting audience elsewhere, and lets the misplaced confidence remain visible for one readable beat. Redirect the whole presentation with an expansive turn when the comic timing calls for it; do not reduce the joke to a tiny embarrassed glance.
- **A strong public objection that becomes personal:** At the planned meeting, the speaker projects the opening challenge to the room, then directs the consequential line to the one person who must answer it. Let the volume change with that narrowing of address while the posture remains committed; preserve the director's chosen force rather than automatically ending in collapse.

## Let people listen and interrupt

The scene continues while a character is silent. Decide who can hear, see or understand the relevant cue and what response the scene needs from them. An engaged listener may hold their position, refuse eye contact or continue an activity; motion is not required to prove that they are present.

Different people may notice the same event at different times. Base that difference on their attention and knowledge, not on a mechanical sequence that gives everyone a separate reaction slot. Preserve requested shared laughter, surprise in unison, cross-talk and interruption.

For overlap, specify the usable relationship between the voices: the entry word or event, whether the first speaker stops or continues, and which words must be understood. Exact dialogue still belongs to the named speaker. Do not impose silence on a character whom the director wants to interject. Avoid subsecond promises unless the actual workflow has demonstrated that control. Dialogue duration and coverage remain governed by [prompt and shot guide](prompt-and-shot-guide.md) and [dialogue coverage](dialogue-coverage-grammar.md).

During speech, pronunciation already moves the mouth. Put a crucial emotional mouth change where it is compatible with the line, or carry the intention through another readable channel. Do not simultaneously demand closed lips and an uninterrupted spoken sentence. Exact lip synchronization, voice continuity and intelligibility require actual audio/video inspection.

If several people need complicated performances at once, first identify whose action must be legible at each moment. Coverage or a reaction insert may solve the problem, but do not silently remove a requested ensemble interaction or turn overlapping speech into orderly solo lines.

## Inspect the result before choosing a repair

Judge the actual selected interval, not just the prompt or a thumbnail. Role ④ records findings; ① owns changes to dramatic intent, ② owns affected assets, ③ owns the generation instruction, and ⑤ owns the edit choice. A performance can be useful in a shorter interval even if another part fails.

| Observed problem | Check before revising |
|---|---|
| The character seems to know something too early | Whether the reaction begins before the character can perceive or understand the cue; distinguish intentional anticipation |
| The expression is blank or too small | Whether the intended change exists, whether the face is visible enough, and whether a bodily or vocal response would communicate it better |
| Every face reacts identically | Whether attention, knowledge and individual intention differ; preserve deliberate unison |
| The face becomes distorted | Whether too many extreme actions compete, whether the reference is clear, and whether the framing or light hides useful anatomy |
| The line and expression fight each other | Whether articulation, breath, a mouth hold and exact timing demand incompatible actions |
| The character changes between clips | Whether the relevant baseline and selected incoming state were available; check actual picture and sound separately |

Revise the smallest responsible part. If an asset or coverage choice prevents the performance from being visible, more facial adjectives will not fix it. Review a broader set of dependencies only when the evidence shows that they conflict.

## Deliver usable actor direction

Keep coding and analysis out of the image/video prompt. Do not include FACS, numbered AU labels, facial-intensity grades or unexplained temporary character/state identifiers. This public guide does not provide a facial-coding lookup table; write the visible action directly.

Before delivery, make sure the intended action is perceivable in the shot, follows the character's information, remains compatible with speech and preserves the director's requested intensity and interaction. Keep deliberate quietness when it is the chosen performance. Use the bilingual and reference-naming rules in [prompt delivery](prompt-delivery.md); a short acting choice must mean the same thing in both languages.
